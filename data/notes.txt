A quantum computer is a computer that uses quantum phenomena such as superposition and entanglement. Quantum computers can perform some calculations exponentially faster than a comparable classical computer. For example, a large-scale quantum computer could break some widely used encryption schemes or efficiently perform physical simulations. Current quantum computers are experimental and specialized.

The basic unit of information in quantum computing is the qubit (quantum bit), replacing the bit from classical computing.[1] Unlike a classical bit, which is one of two states (a binary), a qubit can exist in a linear combination of two states known as a quantum superposition. Measuring a qubit collapses the superposition into one of the two states with a predictable probability. A quantum computer manipulates qubits, using wave interference to make the desired result more likely. Quantum algorithms are the steps the computer takes to influence these results.

Quantum computers are currently impractical for real-world applications. Creating high-quality qubits is difficult. Qubits can decohere when insufficiently isolated from its environment, introducing noise in the results. Governments have invested heavily in research to develop scalable qubits with longer coherence times and lower error rates. Some quantum devices can outperform any classical computer on narrowly defined tasks, a milestone referred to as quantum advantage or quantum supremacy. These tasks are not necessarily useful for real-world applications. Current demonstrations are scientific milestones rather than indications of usefulness. In December 2024, Google's Willow chip achieved below-threshold error correction, a milestone 30 years in the making. Global government investment in quantum computing reached $10 billion by April 2025.[2]
